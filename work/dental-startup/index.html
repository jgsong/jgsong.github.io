At-Home Oral Health Testing

"...nearly 1.7 million people in the US did not have access to dental clinics within a 30-minute drive, and 24.7 million lived in dental care shortage areas."

In 2024, our client envisioned an at-home oral health test to bring accessible and affordable care to dental deserts. Test takers would prepare their own salive sample and scan their test card with their phone leveraging computer vision to receive an assessment of their dental health and customized advice. The client needed a prototype of the experience to test the efficacy of the test card and raise funding.

Over 6 months, I led design on the end-to-end mobile app experience for test takers to guide them through test taking, an employee web app to review and correct computer vision’s readings of test cards scans to improve accuracy. I scoped work, ideated on design and facilitated client design reviews, managed and coached a college hire junior designer, and partnered with a medical copywriter and the dev team to handoff and provide UX QA.

My team included a fractional product strategist and a technical college hire who I managed and coached to play a product designer role. I partnered with engineering to validate feasibility, handoff features, and provide UX QA.

Our goal was to secure funding by:

  • Creating an a working prototype of the test card experience to pilot and prove out the end-to-end experience
  • Design a feedback loop to train computer vision with real-world tests

The design effort ended before the pilot was conducted but the client won hundreds of thousands in funding from investors ahead of the pilot demoing mockups I created to investors.

DIY Testing

The test was meant to be taken at home so it needed to be easy enough to administer without a dental professional present. I reviewed existing healthcare and wellness experiences to understand common patterns and what users might already be familiar with. Ultimately, we modeled the process after scannable COVID-19 tests since we expected most users to have some familiarity with that experience and could map that onto ours.

The test kit manufacturing was still being developed at this time so we stubbed out the rough process with what we knew was required and filled in details as they became available.

Actionable Advice

Once computer vision analyzed their test card, users would see an oral wellness report of their readings, risk levels and tailored advice. The goal here was to help test takers understand why they received the risk levels they did, and how they could change their habits to improve their score.

Without a dentist at home to explain dental health to users, the advice offered needed to be in everyday language. I partnered with the medical copywriter to use more everyday language and use friendly names alongside scientific names. For example, we paired Glucosyltransferase with its friendly name “Plaque Formation”.

Dental Provider Dashboard Concept

We wanted to explore dental providers as potential market, where dentists could provide tests in a clinic setting so I designed a tablet dashboard concept to help communicate the value of understanding risk levels across patients at a clinic and easily restock tests when low.

Training Computer Vision

We were concerned about computer vision’s ability to accurately read test cards so I designed an admin web app for employees to review a queue of completed tests and make corrections to computer vision’s readings. This feedback would be used to train computer vision and also allow reviewers to identify issues with scan quality during the pilot.

Test card design wasn’t far along enough to know what the exact test card readings would look like at this point so rather than leave the markers blank I researched existing tests in the market to anticipate how each marker would be read.

Defining Pilot Success

Our engagement ended before the pilot began but if we had more time I would have defined success metrics with the client:

  • What scan accuracy rate would we need to reach before we could consider the pilot a success?
  • What guidance might we provide users when scanning their card to improve scan quality?

I partnered with the Engineering Lead to re-theme a Material 3 front-end UI library, React Native Paper, so that our Figma UI kit and front-end components would align to a single source of truth. I created color scales and chose appropriate colors for each of Material's semantic colors. To ease handoff to engineering, I mapped the color theme into a .json file with brand-aligned the could copy and paste into their theme.

Saving Client Funds using Free Fonts

The brand guidelines provided by the client specified two paid license fonts, however the client didn't have a license to use them. They'd cost nearly $1000 to acquire. I put together a quick comparison with free Google Fonts alternative that preserved the original intent. These are not huge expenses, but for a startup still seeking funding, every dollar counted.
